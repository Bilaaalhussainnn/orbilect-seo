## Start with margin

Calculate revenue after discounts and refunds, then subtract variable costs such as product, fulfilment and payment fees. If an order contributes 40 before advertising, 40 is the approximate break-even acquisition cost before other costs. Include management and creative costs when assessing the programme.

### Start with what the order contributes

For ecommerce, subtract product cost, payment processing, variable shipping, expected returns and other relevant variable costs from revenue. That result is contribution before advertising. Compare it with the cost to win the order. For lead generation, work from qualified lead to signed job using measured close rates and contribution per customer. Do not assume that every lead or every attributed purchase represents new business.

## Follow a lead through to a sale

For a lead campaign, estimate the share that qualifies and the share of qualified leads that buys. If ten leads cost 250 and one customer contributes 400 before marketing, the campaign has 150 left before overhead. Small samples can swing sharply, so review a longer period.

### Calculate a break-even threshold you can monitor

Suppose a 100-dollar order contributes 35 dollars before advertising. The break-even acquisition cost is 35 dollars if no other campaign-specific costs apply. Revenue divided by ad spend gives ROAS. At 35% contribution margin, break-even revenue ROAS is roughly 2.86 before other campaign-specific costs. If testing, production or agency fees belong in the analysis, track them separately or include them explicitly in the model.

## Compare reported returns with actual orders

Reconcile platform reports with transaction IDs and CRM outcomes. Distinguish new from returning customers and delayed purchases from immediate ones. Adjust attribution windows and refund treatment consistently before comparing campaigns.

### Reconcile platform reports with actual business outcomes

Match ad costs with order or CRM data and adjust for refunds, duplicates and qualified leads. Use a timeline long enough for purchases and cancellations to settle. Evaluate brand terms separately and check the sensitivity of your conclusion to different attribution windows. When you can, compare areas or periods with a controlled shift in spend to understand incremental effect.

## Start with contribution rather than gross revenue

Write down the revenue retained after product or service delivery costs and expected refunds. Compare advertising against the margin available to fund acquisition. A revenue-based return metric can look healthy while the business loses money on fulfilment and sales handling.

## Trace leads into qualified opportunities

Record which enquiries were valid, qualified, and eventually won. Match the reporting window to the sales cycle. A campaign producing inexpensive leads may be less profitable than one with a higher cost per lead and substantially better qualification or close rates.

## Use a transparent worked calculation

For illustration, 20 qualified leads at a 10% close rate produce two customers. If each contributes $500 before advertising, the available contribution is $1,000. Subtract campaign and management costs. These inputs are example assumptions, not a forecast or an industry benchmark.

## Check measurement before changing bids

Verify conversion definitions, duplicate events, attribution windows, and whether offline outcomes are included appropriately. Review test enquiries and suspicious traffic. Optimizing bids around an incorrectly counted conversion can amplify a reporting mistake instead of improving the underlying business result.

## Set a review rule before scaling

Choose a maximum acceptable acquisition cost and an evidence window that reflects your sales cycle. Monitor cash flow as well as eventual customer value. Increase budget gradually and revisit the assumptions when pricing, margins, audience, or the offer changes.

## Calculate the acquisition ceiling from contribution

Use a transparent example rather than a universal target. If a hypothetical first order contributes $120 after the direct costs you include, acquiring that order for $40 leaves $80 before the other costs excluded from the calculation. State those exclusions explicitly.

Then apply the same method to actual business records. Gross revenue, advertising return and contribution are different measures, so a strong revenue-to-ad-spend ratio does not by itself establish that a campaign is profitable.

## Adjust lead economics for qualification and close rates

For a lead-based business, estimate how many valid leads become paying customers and which costs occur during qualification. A cheap form submission can be expensive acquisition if few submissions match the service or reach a completed sale.

Work backwards from an acceptable customer acquisition cost using your observed close rate, while recording the uncertainty in a small sample. Review spam, duplicates and unreachable contacts separately so they do not inflate the apparent pipeline.

## Reconcile refunds and cancellations before scaling

Check whether reported conversions include orders later refunded, cancelled or rejected. Use a reporting period that gives the relevant transactions time to settle and compare the advertising data with operational records.

Document which values include delivery, discounts or other adjustments. Consistency matters more than selecting the most flattering total, because a budget decision based on inflated order value can become more expensive as the campaign grows.

## Write a scaling rule that the operations team can support

Agree what evidence would justify a higher budget and what conditions would trigger a review. Include lead quality, contribution and the business's ability to fulfil additional demand rather than relying only on a campaign-level efficiency metric.

Increase spend through a deliberate decision process and monitor the additional customers acquired. The marginal results from a larger budget may differ from the historical average, especially when audience reach, auction conditions or service capacity changes.

## Why a strong ROAS can still lose money

A store reports 400 dollars of attributed revenue on 100 dollars of ad spend, or 4× ROAS. If the orders contribute 20% before ads, the contribution is only 80 dollars. The campaign is short 20 dollars before additional campaign costs. The next step is to improve margin, reduce acquisition cost or test a different offer.

Build two versions of the model when repeat purchases matter. Start with contribution on the first order. Then show a repeat-purchase estimate backed by a net revenue cohort and a defined window. Include the risk that repeat orders may arrive later or less often than expected. If campaigns appear profitable only in an optimistic model, keep the pilot small until observed margins and repeat orders support the higher acquisition cost.

Which number answers which decision?

| Metric | Formula or input | Decision supported |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ROAS | Attributed revenue divided by media spend | How efficiently spend converts to recorded revenue |
| CPA | Relevant spend divided by acquisitions | Whether the acquisition cost fits contribution |
| Profit contribution | Contribution before ads minus relevant campaign costs | Whether the work creates value under stated assumptions |

## Common questions

### Should I set target ROAS equal to break-even?

Build in room for uncertainty, operating costs and the profit your business needs. Validate the target against actual margins by product or service.

### How do I estimate a defensible cost per lead?

Multiply contribution per won customer by the measured share of leads that convert into won customers. Adjust for selling and fulfilment costs where relevant.

### Why should branded campaigns be reported separately?

People searching for your brand may already be ready to buy. Separating branded traffic keeps a high reported ROAS from masking performance on campaigns intended to acquire new customers.
