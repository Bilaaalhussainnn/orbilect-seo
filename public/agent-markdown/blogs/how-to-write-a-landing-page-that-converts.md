## Match the promise that brought people there

Use the first screen to state who the page is for, what the offer includes, and what visitors can do next. If an ad promises a fixed-price audit, make the price, scope and eligibility easy to confirm on the page.

### Lead with the promise that brought the visitor

Write down the ad, search or referral that led to the page. Carry its main offer into the heading without repeating every phrase. Explain who the offer is for, what buyers receive and what happens after the click. A quote page for commercial solar should give visitors an idea of project eligibility and what information they will need before opening the form.

What the visitor needs before clicking

1. **Recognise the offer**Carry the promise from the ad into the heading
2. **Resolve doubts**Put relevant details and evidence beside the offer
3. **Take action**Use a clear CTA and a form with a meaningful confirmation

## Address the objections that delay action

Use genuine examples, clear deliverables, relevant testimonials and practical details such as timing or cancellation terms. Place supporting evidence near the claim it backs up. Keep forms short and explain how sensitive details will be used when they are needed.

### Order the proof around real objections

Arrange the page from immediate fit to practical questions and evidence. Use service photos, examples, details about delivery or verifiable outcomes when they help a visitor decide. Name the next action in concrete terms. Keep calls to action consistent. If two buyer groups have different requirements and next steps, consider making distinct landing pages instead of piling every use case into one.

## Test the path to completion

Run the page on a phone and through keyboard navigation. Submit the form, check the confirmation, and verify that the lead reaches the right team. Compare qualified conversion counts by source and adjust the offer, message or form based on what you learn.

### Measure completed actions and their quality

Check the mobile reading order and make sure the button is visible without crowding the page. Keep required form fields to information the next step needs. Test what happens after an error and after a successful submit. Measure qualified submissions or booked calls alongside the form completion rate and try changes that map to a known point of friction.

## Match the opening to the visitor’s intent

State who the offer helps, what it provides, and the next useful action. Use the vocabulary of the campaign or search query without mechanically repeating keywords. A visitor should understand the proposition before encountering a long animation or a detailed company history.

## Place proof beside the relevant claim

Support delivery claims with real examples, clear process information, and verifiable evidence. Distinguish illustrative designs from completed client work. A relevant explanation of how the work is delivered can be more useful than an unrelated badge or an unsupported percentage.

## Ask only for information you can use

Choose form fields around the next sales or service step. Explain what happens after submission and test notification delivery. Shortening a form is not automatically better if the team then lacks the information needed to respond usefully or qualify the request.

## Design for scanning and touch

Use concise sections, clear headings, readable type, and comfortably usable controls. Keep key information available without hover. Test the page with the keyboard and a phone so visual polish does not obscure the offer or prevent someone from reaching the form.

## Evaluate the complete response journey

Measure valid submissions, delivery to the team, response time, and qualified opportunities. Compare page versions using consistent traffic and conversion definitions. A success message should describe what actually happened rather than implying that an email or a booking was completed when it was not.

## Make the promise specific enough to evaluate

Explain who the offer is for, what the customer receives and what happens after the primary action. A broad claim about transformation is harder to assess than a clear service scope and a practical next step.

Use supporting details where the question arises. If customers worry about timing, explain the relevant stages; if suitability is uncertain, describe the requirements. Do not substitute a large badge or dramatic headline for the information needed to make a decision.

## Keep proof attached to the claim it supports

Place a relevant example, genuine testimonial or explanation beside the specific point it helps establish. A testimonial about communication does not prove a performance figure, and a sample design should not be presented as a measured client result.

Check names, permissions and context before publication. Clear attribution helps the reader understand the evidence without forcing them to infer what work was delivered or whether the example represents their situation.

## Design the form for correction as well as submission

Provide visible labels, explain unusual requirements and give useful feedback when a field needs correction. Keep entered information where practical so the visitor does not have to start the task again after one mistake.

Test with keyboard navigation and a phone. The form should still be understandable without placeholder text acting as the only label, and its confirmation should describe the next step without promising a response time the business cannot maintain.

## Match the thank-you experience to the offer

Use the confirmation state to explain what was received and what the customer should expect next. If a booking, download or email confirmation is required, make that next action clear and verify that it actually works.

Pass relevant context into the response process using appropriate, minimal data. A landing page is part of an operational journey; the quality of the handover affects whether a promising submission becomes a useful customer conversation.

## A quote page rewrite example

A security installer replaces a broad headline with a premises-specific offer. It lists the building types it serves, shows an anonymised floor-plan example and offers an assessment with a clear response window. Its form asks for the property type and location so the sales team can qualify enquiries quickly.

Use a short message test before writing several long variants. Show potential customers the heading and first section with the ad hidden. Ask what is being offered, who it is for and what they expect the button to do. Compare their answers with the brief. Rewrite ambiguous claims or calls to action before spending on a larger test, especially for services that require trust, eligibility or a longer buying decision.

Answer the questions that block action

| Visitor question | Where to answer | Useful evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is this for me? | Heading and opening body | Clear audience, offer and location |
| Can I trust the provider? | Near the claim it supports | Relevant case study or transparent process |
| What happens after I click? | Above or inside the form | Required information and realistic response expectations |

## Common questions

### Does a landing page need to be short?

Use enough detail for the decision. A complex or high-value service can need more proof before someone requests a proposal.

### Should I send every advertisement to the homepage?

Use the page that most directly fulfils the ad promise. A dedicated landing page can help when the home page serves a wider audience.

### Where should a landing page put customer proof?

Place evidence near the doubt it addresses. A relevant example can support a specific service claim more effectively than a long collection of testimonials at the bottom.
