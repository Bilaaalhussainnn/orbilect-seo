## Find the biggest gap in the journey

Segment conversion rates by device, landing page and traffic source before drawing conclusions. Test the path yourself from arrival to confirmation. Compare analytics with support questions, search terms and usability sessions to understand what visitors could not find.

### Choose one action and define its denominator

A conversion can be a paid order, qualified form or completed booking. Pick the action that matters commercially and count it consistently. Divide completed conversions by the matching sessions or eligible users, documenting which denominator you chose. Segment by device, channel and page because broad averages can hide which journey is underperforming.

2. **Test a change**Address a specific objection or form problem

## Turn observations into a testable hypothesis

If mobile visitors often leave after seeing a quote form, test whether removing fields the sales team never uses helps qualified submissions. Define a primary metric, quality guardrail, and test duration before changing the page. For low traffic, use usability testing and note the limits of before-and-after comparisons.

### Look for friction where visitors already intend to act

Check message match from ad or result to landing page. Review when costs, returns or eligibility become clear. Try the form or checkout on a phone and watch where errors appear. Use qualitative observations, such as support questions or usability sessions, to develop a hypothesis. An observation suggests what to test and does not establish a causal effect by itself.

## Judge the full outcome

Track completed orders or qualified enquiries alongside conversion rate. A shorter form that doubles submissions while reducing qualified leads can make sales less efficient. Document results and watch for changes in traffic mix before rolling the test across the site.

### Test with a primary metric and a quality check

Change one meaningful part of the journey at a time where practical. Write down the expected effect and what would make you reverse the change. For lead generation, monitor qualified leads or closed sales alongside form completions. If traffic is too low for a controlled test, ship an obvious usability fix and record changes over a comparable period without attributing every movement to the fix.

## Define a conversion that matters

Distinguish a button click from a submitted enquiry and a submitted enquiry from a qualified opportunity. Agree which event answers the business question. Keep a written event definition so marketing, analytics, and sales do not report different actions under the same label.

## Segment before redesigning everything

Compare device, traffic source, landing page, and customer intent. A low overall conversion rate may combine strong and weak journeys. Prioritize the segment with a clear problem and enough evidence rather than changing every page in response to a blended average.

## Inspect friction with a real task

Ask someone unfamiliar with the site to complete an enquiry or purchase. Observe unclear wording, missing information, and form failures. Combine qualitative observations with event data; a funnel chart can show where people leave without explaining why they hesitate.

## Write a measurable experiment hypothesis

State the audience, proposed change, expected behaviour, and guardrail metric. For example, shortening a form might increase submissions while reducing qualification quality. Decide how long and how much evidence the test needs before interpreting a short-term fluctuation as a winner.

## Check revenue and service quality afterwards

Follow the enquiry into the CRM and examine qualification, response time, and eventual value. A design that generates more spam is not a commercial improvement. Keep the experiment log even when the result is inconclusive so future changes build on evidence.

## Separate an interface click from a completed customer task

A button click can show interest without proving that the visitor submitted a valid enquiry, paid for an order or attended an appointment. Define the downstream state before calling an interaction a conversion.

Trace a few complete journeys into the CRM or order system. This helps reveal broken submissions, duplicate events and low-quality enquiries that a front-end dashboard might otherwise count as successful outcomes.

## Compare like-for-like traffic before judging a redesign

Record the devices, channels, offers and audience segments contributing to the comparison period. A change in visitor mix can alter the conversion rate even when the page itself behaves the same way.

Review the relevant segment alongside the overall figure. If a new campaign sends less qualified traffic, the site-wide rate alone cannot show whether the redesign improved the experience for the audience it was intended to help.

## Review the customer response after a form submission

Check how quickly a valid enquiry reaches an appropriate person, what information staff receive and whether the customer understands the next step. A shorter form can improve submissions while creating extra manual work if essential context disappears.

Evaluate both sides of the handover. Useful conversion work reduces unnecessary friction for customers while preserving the information the business needs to respond accurately, qualify the enquiry and deliver the promised service.

## Keep a decision log for experiments that do not win

Document the hypothesis, audience, measurement approach and observed result even when an experiment does not improve the target outcome. Record technical problems and changes in acquisition conditions alongside the final decision.

A well-run inconclusive test can still reveal what to investigate next. Avoid rewriting the story around whichever metric increased; use the agreed success measure and explain the limits of the available evidence.

## A mobile quote form example

A contractor sees mobile visitors reach the enquiry page but rarely finish. The required photo upload interrupts people who are asking a simple question. The team makes it optional and measures completed requests, response time and the proportion the sales team accepts.

If acquisition changed at the same time as the page, record the change before attributing a lift. Traffic arriving from a branded email will usually behave differently from a broad awareness ad. Compare like for like where possible, and use form, call or order logs to verify that the instrumented event still measures the same outcome. Set a short review after release to catch broken steps and a longer review that reflects the sales cycle.

## Common questions

### What is a good conversion rate?

Compare with your own baseline by page, intent and device. Different offers and conversion definitions make generic benchmarks unreliable.

### Is conversion optimization only about design?

It also involves offer clarity, page performance, form logic, fulfilment, trust and how sales teams act on incoming requests.

### What if traffic is too low for an A/B test?

Prioritise clear usability fixes, interview recent customers and review the action in a comparable period. Report what changed and what else might have influenced the result.
