## Define the job before choosing a provider

List which work needs doing, who can change the site, and who will own outcomes. An internal team can develop business context over time. An agency may bring several disciplines sooner. A hybrid can pair internal ownership with external specialist support.

### Decide which capability the business is missing

List the work that consistently gets postponed. A migration may need a technical specialist and developer access. A mature content programme may need an editor and research capacity. A multidisciplinary backlog can justify an agency or an internal team. Set a practical budget, define who can approve changes and ask whether the business can actually implement the work it commissions.

## Compare deliverables and accountability

Ask who will audit, write, implement and verify fixes. Compare access, reporting, handoff documentation and the practical time needed from developers. Ask for examples of how a recommendation became a live change.

### Compare proposed work using the same brief

Ask each candidate to explain which problems they would investigate first, what evidence they need and how they would prioritise changes. Ask who does the work, what the monthly or project deliverables contain and which decisions need your approval. Review examples relevant to your business, separating context and measurement from big performance claims. Keep ownership of the site, analytics and reporting accounts.

## Review impact before renewing

Agree on a baseline and the business actions that matter. Use a regular review to decide which work should stop or expand. Keep control of your domain, analytics and content so you can maintain the work if the relationship changes.

### Agree on a baseline and a handover process

Capture the current site condition, important queries, conversion events and revenue or lead quality where available. Agree on the work owner, meeting cadence and how recommendations become changes. Set review points measured against completed work and meaningful leading or business outcomes. Ask for a handover of content, tracking notes, redirects and decisions when the relationship ends.

## Define the work before choosing the model

List technical fixes, content production, analytics, implementation, and stakeholder coordination. Identify which tasks require ongoing ownership and which need specialist input. A clear scope makes an in-house, agency, or hybrid comparison more useful than comparing retainers with a single salary.

## Check implementation capacity

Ask who will publish content, modify templates, and approve changes. Recommendations have limited value if nobody can act on them. Include developer and reviewer availability in the plan, particularly when the work affects a regulated subject or a complex commerce platform.

## Evaluate the evidence behind a proposal

Ask for the diagnostic method, deliverables, access requirements, and reporting definitions. Treat guaranteed rankings or unspecified link volume cautiously. A useful proposal explains uncertainty, priorities, and how progress will be verified without pretending every external search outcome is controllable.

## Protect ownership and continuity

Keep the business in control of domains, analytics, content, and source code. Define handover responsibilities and access removal in the agreement. A good delivery model should remain understandable if a team member leaves or a supplier relationship ends.

## Review progress against agreed decisions

Track implemented fixes, content quality, search visibility, and qualified commercial outcomes. Separate work completed from results that depend on time or external systems. Use review meetings to choose the next priorities rather than merely reading a dashboard aloud.

## Separate strategic advice from implementation responsibility

Ask who will change the website, write the content, resolve technical issues and validate the live result. A proposal can contain a thorough audit while leaving the business with no capacity to implement it.

Map the work to named roles before choosing a model. An internal team, agency or specialist combination can all be appropriate, but the arrangement should close the gap between a recommendation and a deployed improvement rather than producing an expanding backlog nobody owns.

## Ask for evidence that matches the proposed work

Evaluate examples of the actual services being purchased. A strong visual portfolio does not by itself demonstrate technical diagnosis, and an attractive ranking chart does not explain content quality or lead suitability.

Ask how the provider established the starting point, selected the work and checked the result. Genuine examples should be presented with their context and limits, without asking you to accept a universal ranking promise or an unexplained performance claim.

## Keep accounts and business assets recoverable

Confirm ownership and access for the domain, website, analytics, Search Console and the files or content created during the engagement. Record what happens if the working relationship ends or a named contact leaves.

This is a continuity requirement, not a reason to avoid collaboration. A well-defined arrangement lets specialists do their work while preserving the business's ability to recover access, understand the implementation and transition responsibilities when circumstances change.

## Agree how renewal decisions will be made

Set a review cadence that covers completed work, measured outcomes, unresolved dependencies and the next priorities. Distinguish tasks the provider can control from search-engine decisions and customer-side delays.

Review the usefulness of the engagement against those agreed responsibilities. Renewal should be an informed decision about ongoing needs and delivery, rather than a reaction to one volatile keyword or an attractive report that does not connect to the business's actual objectives.

## Choosing a partner for a site move

A retailer plans a platform migration and already has a capable marketing team. It hires a technical specialist for inventory, redirects and post-launch sampling while internal staff handle product content. Six months later, a separate capacity review determines whether ongoing editorial help is needed.

Ask how work will be handed off between the SEO provider, editors and developers. A strong technical audit is only useful if the business can review and implement its priority findings. Put the expected implementation owner next to each deliverable and decide what access can be granted and revoked. During the pitch, have each team explain one difficult tradeoff in plain language, including which work it would postpone when time is limited.

Match staffing to the work

| Situation | Sensible model | What to require |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One complex technical project | Specialist on a defined scope | Launch checks, ownership and rollback plan |
| Ongoing specialist work across channels | Agency or blended model | Named team, priorities and shared reporting |
| Constant collaboration with product teams | Internal owner with targeted support | Decision access and reliable delivery capacity |

## Common questions

### What should a provider guarantee?

Ask for clarity on work and delivery. Nobody can guarantee a first position in organic search because rankings depend on factors beyond the provider’s control.

### How long should the first review window be?

Set checkpoints for implemented work quickly, then evaluate search and customer outcomes over a period that fits the changes, seasonality and reporting lag.

### What does a useful monthly report include?

Look for work completed, the evidence behind the next priorities and the outcomes you agreed to track. It should identify decisions your team owns and distinguish observations from conclusions.
